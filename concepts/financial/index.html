    Retirement & taxes

    The quiet window: Roth conversions between retirement and RMDs

    There may be a stretch of years when your income is the lowest it will ever be again. What you do with those years can change your tax bill for the rest of your life.

    Nora Ellery, CFP®Horizon Financial · Sample article

    Most people picture their tax bill going down when they retire. For a while, it usually does. The paychecks stop, Social Security may not have started yet, and required withdrawals from retirement accounts are still years away. Taxable income falls into a valley.

    Then, at a certain birthday, the valley ends. Required minimum distributions begin, they are calculated whether you need the money or not, and they stack on top of Social Security and any pension you have. For savers who did everything right — who filled a 401(k) faithfully for thirty years — that arrival can be genuinely unpleasant. A large pre-tax balance is a tax bill that has been growing quietly alongside it.

    The years in between are the planning opportunity. This is the window where a Roth conversion does its best work.

    What a conversion actually is

    A Roth conversion moves money from a pre-tax account — a traditional IRA, SEP, SIMPLE, or in many cases a 401(k) — into a Roth IRA. The amount you convert is added to your ordinary income for that year, and you pay tax on it at your regular rates. There is no special conversion tax rate; it simply stacks on top of everything else you earned.

    In exchange, that money is done being taxed. Qualified withdrawals later are tax-free, and a Roth IRA has no required minimum distributions during the original owner’s lifetime. You are choosing to pay tax now, voluntarily, at a rate you can see — instead of later, involuntarily, at a rate you cannot.

    There is no income limit on conversions and no dollar cap. Anyone can convert any amount in any year.
